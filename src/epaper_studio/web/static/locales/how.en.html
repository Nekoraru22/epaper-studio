From your image to the tag

An e-paper tag does not receive a photo: it receives an exact bitmap, piece by piece, over a radio that only listens for a few milliseconds at a time. This is the whole journey, with the real Gicisky protocol and the numbers of the image you have on screen right now. When you send, the card under the tag shows these same messages as they happen.

Framing to the panel's exact size

The panel has a fixed grid of 400 × 300 pixels (120,000 in total) and accepts nothing else. The image is rotated, scaled with a Lanczos filter (the one that best keeps edges when shrinking) and cropped to the framing you choose by dragging.

Each pixel picks one of three pigments

Electronic ink does not mix colours: every dot is white, black or red, with no greys. To decide which, the image is converted to OKLab, a colour space in which mathematical distance resembles the difference the eye perceives. It is compared with the real colour of each ink (the paper's white is slightly cream and the red is dark), not with ideal RGB.

Intermediate tones are faked with error-diffusion dithering: when a pixel is rounded, the difference that is lost is spread among its not-yet-processed neighbours, so a grey area ends up as a mix of black and white dots in just the right proportion.

First, every colour is projected onto what the panel can paint. A white, black and red panel has a single colour axis, the red one: a green points the opposite way, so it contributes no red at all and is dithered in greys; a purple keeps only its reddish part. Without this, the error of those impossible colours would pile up with no way to compensate and end up spilling as blotches over other parts of the image.

Packing into bit planes

The panel controller knows nothing about colours, only bits. Your tag receives two planes of 1 bit per pixel, one after the other: in the first a 1 is white and a 0 is ink; in the second a 1 is red. A red dot is therefore 0 in the first plane and 1 in the second. Rows go from top to bottom and each byte holds eight consecutive pixels, the leftmost in the highest bit. Finding the tag: the advert

The tag spends almost all of its time asleep. Every so often it powers the radio for an instant, sends an advert and goes back to sleep; that is why it comes and goes while scanning. The model comes from joining the first and last bytes and keeping 14 bits: 0x004B, a white, black and red EPD 4.2" of 400 × 300. With that the program knows the panel's exact size and how to pack the image.

Connection and services

This program, acting as the central, waits for one of those adverts and answers in that same window with a connection request. Once connected, it walks the tag's GATT table looking for service fef0. Inside there are two characteristics that matter: commands go out and replies come back on fef1, which the tag sends as notifications; the image data goes on fef2.

First the MTU, the maximum size of each message, is negotiated. Without negotiation it is 23 bytes, which would not be enough here: each block is 244 bytes plus a 3-byte header, so an MTU of at least 247 is needed. BlueZ negotiates it on its own when connecting.

Three commands to start

All of them are written to fef1 without asking for a protocol-level acknowledgement: the answer arrives as a notification, and the program does not send the next one until it gets it.

The image, block by block

The image is cut into blocks of 240 bytes. Each one is written to fef2 preceded by its number in 4 bytes, and the tag answers on fef1 with 05 00 and the number of the block it wants next. The tag sets the pace: if a block arrives damaged, it asks for the same number again and the program repeats it. When it asks for one past the end, the image is complete. The estimate assumes about 30 ms per block: one write and one notification back, each in its own connection event. What takes longest is almost always the part before: waiting for the advert and connecting.

Refresh and back to sleep

With the buffer complete, the program disconnects and the tag refreshes the panel: it applies a sequence of voltages that first clears the previous image (the black and white flashing) and then sets the pigments. Red is the slowest to move, so on panels with red it takes around 15 seconds.

Then it goes back to sleep and the image stays put without using any power: e-paper only consumes energy when it changes. That is why a coin cell lasts for years, and why the program warns you if you try to send the image the tag is already showing.
